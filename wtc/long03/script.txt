It's twenty degrees outside. You start your car, walk back inside, and finish your coffee while it warms up for ten minutes. Your dad did it. His dad did it. And it feels like the responsible thing to do. But here's the truth. For almost any car built in the last thirty years, those ten minutes did more harm than good. You burned fuel, you went nowhere, and your engine still isn't fully warm. So where did this habit come from, and what should you actually do on a cold morning? And if you drive a diesel, stay to the end, because the rules are different for you. The habit comes from carburetors. A carburetor mixes fuel and air with simple mechanics, and when it's cold, fuel doesn't vaporize well. So a cold carbureted engine would stumble, hesitate and stall until it warmed up. Back then, warming up was necessary. But by the mid nineties, every new car sold in America had fuel injection. Today a computer reads sensors, like engine temperature and air temperature, and adjusts the fuel many times per second. Your engine knows it's cold, and it handles it for you. So what happens when you let a modern gas engine idle in the cold? First, it warms up slowly. At idle the engine makes very little heat, so it takes much longer to reach operating temperature than if you just drove. Second, while it's cold, the computer runs a richer mixture, meaning extra fuel. Some of that fuel doesn't burn. It can wash oil off the cylinder walls and end up diluting your engine oil. The longer you idle cold, the longer this lasts. Third, your catalytic converter doesn't clean the exhaust until it gets hot, so a cold idling car is at its dirtiest. And fourth, idling only warms the engine. Your transmission, wheel bearings, steering and suspension only warm up when the car moves. On top of that, a typical car burns about a quarter to half a gallon of fuel per hour at idle. Ten minutes every morning adds up to several gallons over a winter, for zero miles. What about the oil? Isn't it too thick when it's cold? Modern multigrade oil is made for this. The W in zero W twenty stands for winter, and a low number means the oil flows well in the cold. Within seconds of starting, oil pressure builds and the oil reaches the parts that need it. So here's the right way for a gas car. Start the engine. Buckle up, check your mirrors, and give it about thirty seconds. Then drive away gently. For the first five to ten minutes, no hard acceleration and no high revs. Driving is the fastest way to warm up the engine, and you'll get heat in the cabin sooner too. If it's brutally cold, around zero degrees Fahrenheit or below, give it a minute or two, and check your owner's manual, because some carmakers give specific cold weather advice. In places that cold, an engine block heater makes a big difference. Now, diesels. A diesel has no spark plugs. It ignites the fuel with the heat of compressed air. So when it's cold, starting is harder, and the rules change. Step one. If your diesel uses a key, turn it to on and wait for the glow plug light, the little coil symbol, to go out. Glow plugs heat the cylinders so the engine can fire. Then crank. Many push button diesels do this for you automatically. If it doesn't start, don't crank for more than about fifteen seconds at a time, and let the starter rest before you try again. Step two. Don't idle it for long. A diesel idles on very little fuel, so it makes even less heat than a gas engine. In deep cold, an idling diesel can take a very long time to warm up. Meanwhile, fuel that doesn't burn completely can build up as a black, oily residue in the exhaust. Mechanics call this wet stacking. It can also dilute the oil and load up the diesel particulate filter, the DPF, with soot. Some trucks have a high idle feature to warm up faster, but even truck owner's manuals warn that long idling can keep the exhaust system from cleaning itself. So give it a short idle until it runs smoothly, then drive gently with a light load. Step three. Use the block heater. Many diesel trucks come with one built in. Look for a plug tucked near the grille or the front bumper. Plug it in two to four hours before you drive, and a cheap outdoor timer saves electricity. Your manual tells you at what temperature to use it. Step four. Watch the fuel. Diesel contains wax. As it gets cold, that wax forms crystals that can clog the fuel filter, and the engine starves. Depending on the blend, regular number two diesel can start clouding anywhere from around freezing down to the teens. Fuel stations in cold areas sell winter blend diesel. If you use an anti-gel additive, add it before the cold hits, ideally right before you fill up so it mixes in. Anti-gel prevents gelling. It usually won't fix fuel that has already gelled. A few rules for everyone. Take the time to clear all the ice and snow from your windshield, windows, mirrors, lights and roof. That's the one good reason to let the car run longer, with the defroster on. Never warm up a car inside a garage, even with the door open. Carbon monoxide can build up fast, and you can't smell it. And don't leave your running car unattended. Thieves look for exactly that, and in some states it's illegal on public streets. If you want a warm cabin, a remote starter keeps the doors locked. So here's the short version. Gas car: start it, buckle up, thirty seconds, drive gently. Diesel: wait for the glow plug light, plug in the block heater, short idle, drive gently, and keep winter fuel in the tank. Your grandpa's car needed ten minutes.
