On 18 October 1965, a helicopter disappears over the jungle of Vietnam.
On board is a captain of the American Special Forces: Larry Thorne.
Only 34 years later is the crash site found.
Yet this man wore three different uniforms in his life.
He fought for Finland against the Soviet Union, he served in the Waffen-SS, and he died as an officer of the US Army.
In his homeland he was known by another name: Lauri Törni.
Lauri Allan Törni was born on 28 May 1919 in Viipuri, a port city in Karelia.
At the time it belonged to Finland; today it is called Vyborg and lies in Russia.
In 1938 he was called up for military service.
A year later, in November 1939, the Soviet Union attacked Finland.
In the Winter War, Törni fought north of Lake Ladoga, where Finnish units destroyed encircled Soviet troops near Lemetti.
When the war ended in March 1940, he was a reserve lieutenant.
Finland had to give up large parts of Karelia, including his hometown of Viipuri.
Finland now sought support from Germany.
In June 1941, Törni travelled to Vienna and completed several weeks of training with the Waffen-SS there.
That same summer he returned to Finland, because the Continuation War was beginning there.
In 1943 he was given command of a small unit that was soon simply called Detachment Törni.
His men operated far behind Soviet lines, laid ambushes and often returned only after days.
According to reports, the Soviet side put a bounty of three million Finnish marks on him.
In the summer of 1944 he fought at Ilomantsi, in one of the last great battles of this war.
One of the soldiers under his command was Mauno Koivisto, who decades later became President of Finland.
In July 1944, Törni received the Mannerheim Cross, Finland's highest military decoration.
In September 1944, Finland concluded an armistice with the Soviet Union.
For Törni, the war was not over.
He joined a group that wanted to resist a Soviet occupation, and in January 1945 he went to Germany to be trained as a saboteur.
In the last weeks of the war he fought in a Waffen-SS unit in northern Germany and surrendered to British troops in May 1945.
He escaped from the prison camp near Lübeck and made his way back to Finland.
There the state police arrested him.

He was sentenced to six years in prison for treason.
Twice he escaped from prison, twice he was caught again.
In December 1948, President Juho Paasikivi pardoned him.
But Törni no longer saw a future in Finland.
In 1949 he went to Sweden and signed on under a false name aboard a ship bound for Venezuela.
From there he sailed as a seaman on a freighter heading for the USA.
In the Gulf of Mexico, off the coast of Alabama, he jumped overboard and swam ashore.
A new law opened a path for him: the Lodge Act allowed foreigners to serve in the US Army and thereby gain citizenship.
In 1954, Lauri Törni joined the American army under the name Larry Thorne.
He joined the Special Forces, the Green Berets, became a citizen and eventually an officer.
In 1962, operating from Bad Tölz, he led a recovery mission in Iran.
At an altitude of around 3,800 metres in the Zagros Mountains, his team recovered the dead of a crashed US aircraft and classified material, after earlier search parties had failed.
From 1963, Thorne was deployed in Vietnam.
In 1965 he belonged to a secret unit tasked with bringing reconnaissance teams across the border into Laos.
On 18 October 1965 he took off from Kham Duc in a helicopter of the South Vietnamese Air Force.
In bad weather the aircraft crashed into a forested mountain area southwest of Da Nang.
The search was unsuccessful.
Thorne was listed as missing and was later promoted to major.
Only in 1999 did an American-Vietnamese team find the crash site and his remains.
On 26 June 2003 he was buried at Arlington National Cemetery, under the name Larry Thorne.
Three uniforms, two names and a life between the fronts.
In Finland, many still regard Lauri Törni as a war hero, while others point to his service in the Waffen-SS.
His story shows how closely the fates of individual people in the 20th century were bound up with the great conflicts.
