Most people believe the Second World War began on 1 September 1939, when German tanks crossed the Polish border.
Technically, that is true: on that day the first shots were fired.
But the war itself, its machinery, the anger behind it, the economic desperation that drove it and the political blindness that made it possible, all of that had been set in motion decades earlier.
Wars do not come out of nowhere.
They are built, piece by piece, decision by decision, over years and sometimes over generations.
The Second World War, the deadliest conflict in human history, was no exception.
It was assembled like a bomb, component by component, by politicians, bankers, generals and diplomats who either did not understand what they were building or simply did not care.
Anyone who really wants to understand why more than 60 million people died between 1939 and 1945 has to turn the clock back much further than 1939. Back to the end of the First World War.
Back to a single document, signed in a single hall of a single palace.
Because the true origin of the Second World War was written in ink long before it was written in blood.
It is 28 June 1919. The place is the Hall of Mirrors in the Palace of Versailles, just outside Paris.
A vast room, gilded and gleaming, built to display the power and grandeur of France.
On this day it is packed with diplomats, politicians and officers from all over Europe and beyond.
They have come to sign the Treaty of Versailles, the document meant to officially end the First World War.
At first glance, the treaty was supposed to bring peace.
It was meant to put order into the devastation left by four years of industrialised killing.
The war had cost around 17 million lives, soldiers and civilians alike.
It had wiped out entire landscapes and toppled empires.
Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, the Russian Empire and the German Empire had collapsed.
Europe lay in ruins, and its statesmen wanted at any price to prevent anything like it from ever happening again.
But the men who wrote the Treaty of Versailles were thinking less about a lasting peace.
They were thinking about punishment.
And the country they wanted to punish above all was Germany.
The treaty contained an article numbered 231. It would become one of the most consequential sentences in modern history.
In essence, it said that Germany and its allies bore full responsibility for the outbreak of the war.
This was no diplomatic formality.
It was a moral verdict that declared an entire nation guilty.
The German delegation was not allowed to negotiate.
It was not allowed to change anything.
They were given a choice: sign, or the naval blockade continues, the blockade that was already starving German civilians.
They signed.
And with that they lit a fuse that would burn for twenty years.
The terms of the treaty went far beyond an admission of guilt.
Germany had to give up around 13 percent of its territory in Europe, more than 70,000 square kilometres, and with it about a tenth of its population.
Between six and seven million people suddenly lived outside Germany's borders.
Alsace-Lorraine returned to France after almost five decades of German rule.
Parts of West Prussia and Upper Silesia went to the newly founded Polish state.
This cut East Prussia off from the rest of the Reich through the so-called Polish Corridor.
The heavily industrialised Saar region with its coal was placed under League of Nations administration for 15 years, and its mines went to France.
North Schleswig went to Denmark, Eupen and Malmedy to Belgium.
The port city of Danzig, whose population was almost entirely German, became a Free City under international supervision.
And outside Europe, Germany lost all of its colonies, territories of almost three million square kilometres in Africa, Asia and the Pacific.
They were divided among the victorious powers, mainly between Britain and France.
But the territorial losses were only the beginning.
The treaty also imposed drastic military restrictions on Germany.
The once mighty German army was cut to no more than 100,000 men.
The navy was reduced to a remnant fleet, and an air force was banned altogether.
Tanks, heavy artillery and submarines were forbidden.
The Rhineland, the strip of land along the western border with France, was permanently demilitarised.
No German soldiers, no fortifications, no weapons of any kind.
The western flank of the Reich was left completely exposed.
And then came the reparations.
In 1921 a reparations commission set the sum Germany was to pay for war damages: 132 billion gold marks, about 33 billion dollars at the time.
A sum so enormous that economists openly said at the time it could not be collected without shaking the entire world financial system.
It was to be paid in gold and foreign currency, in raw materials, merchant ships, coal, livestock and industrial goods, and over decades.
Germany did not just owe money.
It owed its economic future.
Imagine how this looked from the German point of view.
A brutal war had just ended.
Millions of young men were dead or maimed.
The economy was in ruins.
The Kaiser had abdicated and fled to the Netherlands.
The country was governed by a young, fragile democracy, the Weimar Republic, which many Germans had never wanted and even more openly despised.
And now they were told the whole war was Germany's fault.
That they should hand over land, colonies, coal, ships, their army and money.
And those who demanded it allowed no negotiation, no objection, not even a seat at the table.
Sign or starve.
The psychological effect on the Germans can hardly be overstated.
The treaty did not just weaken the country materially.
It humiliated it to the core.
In Germany it was called the Diktat, a dictated peace rather than a negotiated one.
Many Germans sincerely believed the war had never been lost on the battlefield.
At the armistice, German troops were still standing on foreign soil.
From this grew the so-called stab-in-the-back myth: the claim that the army had been betrayed from within, by politicians, communists and others who had given up too early.
It was a conspiracy story, but a powerful one.
And it would be ruthlessly exploited in the years to come.
The Weimar Republic now found itself in an almost impossible position.
It was born of defeat, tied to surrender and burdened with an economy collapsing under reparations, debt and lost industry.
The government responded to its growing obligations by printing money, enormous amounts of money.
Germany had to pay its reparations in gold or foreign currency, but had far too little of either.
So the Reichsbank ran the printing presses at an unprecedented pace, flooding the economy with paper marks backed by nothing.
At first the effects remained manageable.
Prices rose, but slowly, and wages more or less kept up.
Then everything spun out of control.
In January 1923, one US dollar was worth about 17,000 marks.
That was already a catastrophic rate, but it would get far worse.
In July, a dollar cost more than 350,000 marks.
By the end of October it was more than 70 billion.
And in November 1923, a single American dollar was worth 4.2 trillion marks.
4.2 trillion.
In everyday life this meant workers were paid twice a day, because their wages lost half their value between morning and evening.
People pushed wheelbarrows full of banknotes to the baker to buy a loaf of bread.
Savings that had taken whole lifetimes to build vanished overnight.
A pensioner who had carefully saved for a secure old age suddenly could not buy even a postage stamp with his entire fortune.
Restaurants stopped printing prices on their menus because the numbers changed by the hour.
Some shops stopped accepting cash at all.
People burned banknotes for heat, because the paper was worth more as fuel than as money.
The social fabric of Germany began to come apart.
The middle class, the backbone of every stable democracy, was practically wiped out.
Academics, teachers, civil servants, small business owners, all those who had followed the rules, saved and trusted their institutions, were now left with nothing.
And this is the crucial point: it was not the very poor or the very rich who radicalised.
It was the middle class.
People who had something to lose, then lost everything, and who were now desperately looking for someone to blame and someone to follow.
It was at exactly this time, in November 1923, that a then little-known political agitator named Adolf Hitler made his first grab for power.
With a few hundred followers he stormed a beer hall in Munich to launch a coup against the Bavarian state government.
The attempt was amateurish, chaotic and collapsed within a few hours.
Hitler was arrested, tried and sentenced to five years of fortress confinement, of which he served less than nine months.
In prison at Landsberg he dictated a book setting out his political ideology, his deep resentment of the Treaty of Versailles, his conspiracy theories about who was to blame for Germany's humiliation, and his apocalyptic vision of the future.
The book was called Mein Kampf.
The blueprint for everything that followed was laid out in it for anyone who cared to read it.
But at the time hardly anyone did.
At this point everything could still have turned out differently.
In 1924 hyperinflation was brought under control with the introduction of a new currency, the Rentenmark.
In the same year the Dawes Plan came into force, named after the American banker Charles Dawes, who had chaired the committee.
The plan was meant to stabilise the German economy and get reparation payments moving again.
It spread Germany's payments over more manageable annual instalments.
Above all, though, it opened the floodgates to huge American loans.
Over the following years, American banks and financial houses lent Germany billions of dollars.
Wall Street firms such as J.P.
Morgan, Dillon, Read and the National City Bank issued huge bonds to finance German industry.
The money flowed into chemical groups like IG Farben, steel giants like Vereinigte Stahlwerke and many other companies.
At first glance this looked like a rescue.
And in many ways it worked.
The economy stabilised, industrial output rose, unemployment fell and cultural life flourished.
The years from about 1924 to 1929 went down in history as the Golden Twenties.
Berlin became one of the liveliest cities in Europe, a centre of art, film, music and intellectual life.
The Weimar Republic finally seemed to find its footing.
Democracy, it seemed, might really take root in Germany.
But inside this apparent boom lay a fatal flaw, and it was structural.
Germany's recovery depended almost entirely on a constant inflow of American capital.
The loans did not build an economy that could stand on its own.
They built a house of cards.
German companies and municipalities borrowed large sums from American banks, and part of that borrowed money went into reparation payments to France and Britain.
France and Britain in turn used those reparations to repay their own war debts to the United States.
So the money went round in a circle.
American banks lent money to Germany, Germany paid France and Britain, and France and Britain paid the money back to the American banks.
And the cycle started all over again.
Everyone was borrowing, and no one was building anything that could survive without new credit.
If American capital ever dried up, the whole structure would collapse.
In 1929 it did collapse, and spectacularly.
On 24 October 1929 a crash began on the New York Stock Exchange that wiped out billions of dollars within days.
It set off a chain reaction of bank failures, business closures and economic collapse that went down in history as the Great Depression, the worst economic crisis of the modern era.
In the United States the consequences were devastating.
By 1933 around 15 million Americans were unemployed, more than 20 percent of the workforce.
But Germany was hit even harder.
American banks urgently needed money and immediately called in their short-term loans from Europe.
For Germany, which had become completely dependent on those loans, it was the economic death blow.
The golden years vanished almost overnight.
Banks collapsed, factories closed, businesses went bankrupt.
At the end of 1929 about 1.5 million Germans were unemployed.
By the end of 1930 the number had more than doubled to almost four million.
And by early 1933 it was six million, around 30 percent of the workforce.
In some industrial regions the rate was even higher.
Families were thrown out of their homes.
The queues outside the soup kitchens stretched along entire streets.
The number of suicides rose.
Despair was everywhere.
And once again the middle class was hit hardest.
The same people who had been ruined by hyperinflation only a few years earlier, and had only just recovered, were now ruined a second time.
This time many of them had had enough.
They had lost faith in the Weimar Republic, in capitalism and in democracy itself.
And they were ready to follow anyone who promised order, dignity and national strength.
This was the moment Adolf Hitler had been waiting for.
His NSDAP had been a fringe party during the Golden Twenties.
Now it suddenly found a huge audience.
In the 1928 Reichstag election it had won just 2.6 percent of the vote.
But in September 1930, at the first election after the crisis began, that figure shot up to 18.3 percent.
The NSDAP was now the second largest party in Germany.
By July 1932, with 37 percent, it was the largest.
And on 30 January 1933 Adolf Hitler was appointed Reich Chancellor.
In economic terms, something remarkable had happened here.
The same financial system that was meant to stabilise Germany through the Dawes Plan had unintentionally created the conditions for its destruction.
American capital had flowed into Germany, created artificial prosperity, built dependence and then vanished.
What remained was an economic wasteland, fertile ground for the most destructive political movement of modern history.
This was not a conspiracy.
It was negligence on a historic scale.
But it was not only in Germany that the interwar years were preparing future conflicts.
To understand the full scale of what led to the Second World War, you have to look east, to Japan.
In the 1920s and 1930s Japan was a country in upheaval.
In the preceding decades it had industrialised rapidly and risen to become one of the strongest military and economic powers in Asia.
In the First World War Japan had fought on the side of the Allies and expected to be treated as an equal partner in the postwar order.
Instead it felt deeply slighted.
At the Paris Peace Conference the Western powers rejected Japan's proposal to include a racial equality clause in the Covenant of the League of Nations.
The message was clear: as a military ally Japan was useful, but as a true equal it was not recognised by the Western colonial powers.
The Great Depression hit Japan hard, especially in the countryside.
Silk exports, a vital source of income for Japanese farmers, collapsed when American demand disappeared.
Millions of farming families fell into poverty, and food grew scarce.
The political leadership, seen as corrupt and incompetent, increasingly lost control.
Into this vacuum stepped the Japanese military, which had grown ever more powerful and nationalistic during the 1920s.
On 18 September 1931, officers of Japan's Kwantung Army staged a false flag incident near the city of Mukden in Chinese Manchuria.
They set off a small explosive charge on the South Manchuria Railway, blamed Chinese saboteurs and used it as a pretext for a full-scale invasion.
Within a few months the Japanese army conquered the entire region and set up a puppet state called Manchukuo, with an emperor as figurehead, fully controlled from Tokyo.
This was an open attack on another member of the League of Nations.
China asked the League of Nations for help.
The League sent a commission of inquiry led by the British diplomat Lord Lytton.
The commission took more than a year to produce its report.
It stated that Japan's actions were not justified and that Chinese sovereignty over Manchuria had to be restored.
Japan's answer was simple and devastating: it left the League of Nations and stayed in Manchuria.
And this is the decisive point.
The League of Nations did absolutely nothing to stop Japan.
It had no army and no means of enforcing its decisions.
The United States, the most powerful country in the world, was not even a member.
Britain and France, the two most influential members, were caught up in their own economic crises and had no interest in confronting Japan on the other side of the world.
So the League published its report, Japan ignored it, and the world carried on as usual.
This was more than a failure of international diplomacy.
It was a signal, loud and clear, to every expansionist power in the world: the international system meant to prevent wars was in truth completely toothless.
If Japan could invade a sovereign state, defy the League of Nations and suffer no consequences at all, then others could too.
In Italy, Benito Mussolini was watching very closely.
Mussolini had ruled Italy as a fascist dictator since 1922 and dreamed of restoring the glory of the Roman Empire.
His eye fell on Ethiopia, one of the last independent states in Africa.
Of all places, it was Ethiopia that had defeated an Italian invasion army in 1896, a humiliation that still rankled in Italy.
In October 1935 Mussolini launched his attack.
The Ethiopian emperor Haile Selassie desperately asked the League of Nations for help.
He travelled to Geneva in person and gave one of the most moving speeches in diplomatic history, imploring the assembled delegates to honour their promise of collective security.
The League imposed economic sanctions on Italy, but they were deliberately watered down.
Oil, coal and steel, exactly what Italy needed for its war, were expressly excluded.
Britain and France feared driving Mussolini into Hitler's arms and secretly negotiated the Hoare-Laval Pact, a backroom deal that would have handed Italy two thirds of Ethiopia in exchange for peace.
When the plan leaked to the press, there was a scandal.
But it revealed the uncomfortable truth: the Western democracies cared more about keeping good relations with dictators than about defending the principles they claimed to stand for.
In May 1936 Italian troops took Addis Ababa.
Ethiopia was completely conquered.
The League of Nations had failed again, and this time the failure weighed even heavier than in Manchuria, because it happened before the eyes of Europe and not on the other side of the world.
Every dictator on the continent could see that the system meant to prevent aggression was not only weak.
It was even willing to sacrifice smaller states to appease larger ones.
Meanwhile in Germany, Hitler followed all of this with the attention of a predator studying its territory.
And step by step he tore the Treaty of Versailles apart.
As early as October 1933 he had taken Germany out of the League of Nations.
In 1935 he openly announced the reintroduction of conscription and the building of a new German air force, both a direct breach of the treaty.
The reaction from London and Paris was strikingly restrained.
In June 1935 Britain even concluded the Anglo-German Naval Agreement, which allowed Germany a navy of up to 35 percent of the Royal Navy.
The agreement was made without consulting France and effectively legitimised the violation of the military terms of Versailles.
For Hitler the message was unmistakable: the Western powers were willing to negotiate away the treaty's restrictions rather than enforce them.
Then came 7 March 1936. On that day Hitler ordered German troops into the Rhineland, the demilitarised zone the Treaty of Versailles had created as a buffer between Germany and France.
It was an enormous gamble.
At that point the Wehrmacht was still relatively small and poorly equipped.
Hitler himself later admitted that the 48 hours after the march in were the most nerve-racking of his life.
His commanders had orders to withdraw immediately if the French army responded militarily.
But France did nothing.
The French government was paralysed.
It consulted Britain, and London advised against any military response.
The British view, in essence, was that Germany was only walking into its own back garden.
And so the moment passed.
Hitler's gamble paid off.
He had taken back the Rhineland without firing a shot, and he had proven something far more important: the Western democracies would not fight to defend the postwar order.
They would protest, condemn and wring their hands, but they would not act.
This emboldened Hitler beyond measure.
Every provocation that went unanswered made the next one easier.
Every line crossed without consequences made the next one seem less important.
In this slow game of testing and escalating, Hitler refined the strategy that would carry him to the brink of war.
At the same time another conflict was unfolding, one sometimes called the dress rehearsal for the Second World War.
In July 1936 a group of officers that included General Francisco Franco tried to overthrow Spain's democratically elected Republican government.
The country split in two, and a brutal civil war began that would last almost three years.
What made the Spanish Civil War so significant on the road to world war was foreign involvement.
Hitler and Mussolini immediately backed Franco's Nationalists, sending troops, aircraft, tanks and ammunition.
Germany's Condor Legion gained valuable combat experience in Spain, testing tactics, weapons and methods that would be used on a far larger scale only a few years later.
The destruction of the Basque town of Guernica by German and Italian bombers in April 1937 became one of the most harrowing episodes of the war.
A deliberate attack on civilians that foreshadowed the bombing campaigns of the coming world war.
The Western democracies meanwhile relied on non-intervention and officially supplied weapons to neither side.
This policy was a fig leaf.
Germany and Italy supplied Franco quite openly while Britain and France looked on.
The Soviet Union did support the Republic, but irregularly and not without conditions.
The Spanish Republic was abandoned by exactly the democracies that should have been its natural allies.
Spain became the laboratory of modern war, the testing ground for the weapons, tactics and alliances that would shape the Second World War.
And it confirmed the pattern already seen in Manchuria, Ethiopia and the Rhineland.
Aggression was rewarded.
Inaction was the rule.
And the institutions meant to prevent wars would not or could not act.
By 1938 the pattern was set, and Hitler was ready for his boldest move yet.
In March German troops marched into Austria.
The so-called Anschluss made Austria part of the Greater German Reich.
Once again the Western powers protested, and once again they did not act.
Austria, a German-speaking country with a strong National Socialist following, was absorbed into the Third Reich without a single shot.
Then came the Sudeten crisis.
The Sudetenland was a mountainous, industrialised region in western Czechoslovakia where more than three million Germans lived.
Hitler demanded that the territory be handed to Germany, citing the alleged oppression of the German-speaking population.
Czechoslovakia was a democracy, well armed and ready to fight.
It had a modern army, strong border fortifications and mutual assistance treaties with France and the Soviet Union.
But it would never get the chance to defend itself.
In September 1938 British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain flew to Munich to negotiate directly with Hitler.
With him came French Prime Minister Édouard Daladier and the Italian dictator Benito Mussolini.
Czechoslovakia was not invited.
On 30 September the four powers reached agreement.
Germany was allowed to occupy the Sudetenland immediately.
Czechoslovakia's objections were brushed aside.
Its border fortifications, its industry and its strategic position in Central Europe were sacrificed on the altar of appeasement.
Chamberlain flew back to London, waved a sheet of paper and announced that he had achieved peace for our time.
The crowd cheered him, desperate to believe that war had been averted.
But not everyone was convinced.
Winston Churchill, then an ordinary member of parliament, delivered a damning verdict.
Britain and France had been given the choice between dishonour and war.
They had chosen dishonour, and they would get war anyway.
Churchill was proved right.
Only six months later, in March 1939, German troops marched into what remained of Czechoslovakia and occupied the whole country.
The Munich Agreement, meant to secure peace, was waste paper before the year was out.
Hitler had never intended to keep it.
It had only been a tool to eliminate Czechoslovakia without having to fight for it.
At this point something changed in the calculations of the Western powers.
The occupation of Czechoslovakia was different from everything before.
The Rhineland, Austria and even the Sudetenland could be presented, however thinly, as the recovery of territories that were ethnically or historically German.
The occupation of Czechoslovakia, by contrast, was the conquest of a foreign people.
It was naked imperialism, and it finally destroyed the illusion that Hitler's ambition could be satisfied through negotiation.
Britain and France gave guarantees to Poland, promising to defend its independence if it were attacked.
But by then the strategic situation had already deteriorated dramatically.
The Rhineland was remilitarised, Austria annexed, Czechoslovakia broken up.
The League of Nations was an empty shell.
And for almost a decade the Western democracies had shown that they would sacrifice smaller states to avoid a confrontation.
Then came the last piece of the puzzle, the event that made the attack on Poland not just likely but inevitable.
On 23 August 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union shocked the world with a non-aggression pact, the Hitler-Stalin Pact, also called the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact after the two countries' foreign ministers.
Officially it was a simple agreement not to attack each other.
But a secret additional protocol divided Eastern Europe into a German and a Soviet sphere of interest.
Poland was to be split between them.
With the Soviet Union removed as a threat, Hitler had a free hand.
Nine days later, on 1 September 1939, the Wehrmacht invaded Poland.
Two days after that, Britain and France declared war on Germany.
The Second World War had officially begun.
But as we have seen, the war did not begin on 1 September 1939. That day merely saw the explosion of twenty years of failure, resentment and misjudgement.
The true origins of the Second World War reach back through the entire interwar period.
Through the appeasement of dictators, through the collapse of collective security, through the Great Depression and the artificial prosperity before it, through the hyperinflation that destroyed a whole generation's savings, through the harsh reparations that crippled Germany's economy, and all the way back to a single document signed in a gilded hall at Versailles.
And that is exactly what makes this story so disturbing: at almost every stage the catastrophe could have been avoided.
Had the Treaty of Versailles been less harsh, Germany might not have been driven into such resentment.
Had the Dawes Plan built a truly independent economy instead of creating dependence on foreign loans, the Great Depression might not have hit Germany so devastatingly.
Had the League of Nations stood up to Japan in Manchuria, Mussolini might have hesitated to attack Ethiopia.
Had France marched into the Rhineland in 1936, when it was militarily far superior, Hitler's career might have ended there.
And had Britain and France stood by Czechoslovakia in 1938 instead of giving it up at Munich, the war might never have broken out.
But they did not.
At every turning point, those who could have prevented the catastrophe chose the path of least resistance.
They chose short-term calm over long-term security.
They chose appeasement over confrontation.
They chose the illusion of peace over real deterrence.
And in doing so they made possible the greatest catastrophe in human history.
There is a deeper lesson in this story, one that reaches far beyond the 1920s and 1930s.
It is about the interplay between economics, politics and the conditions under which societies become vulnerable to extremism.
Many authoritarian movements in history have followed a similar pattern.
First comes the economic crisis, then national humiliation, then the search for scapegoats, and finally the strongman who promises to restore lost greatness.
In Germany this pattern played out with devastating precision, but to varying degrees it also appeared in Italy, Japan and Spain.
Economic despair, combined with a sense of national humiliation and a loss of faith in democratic institutions, created the conditions in which authoritarian leaders could come to power and lead their countries into war.
And the international financial system did not prevent this; it often made it worse.
The cycle of loans, reparations and debt repayments created a fragile web of mutual dependence that carried economic shocks across borders at devastating speed.
When Wall Street collapsed in 1929, it did not only hit the American economy.
It destroyed the German economy, which in turn propped up the French and British economies, which in turn financed the American banking system.
The whole thing was a closed circuit, and when one link broke, the whole chain snapped.
That raises perhaps the most uncomfortable question of all: who was really responsible for the Second World War?
The simple answer is Adolf Hitler.
And without Hitler's fanaticism the war would certainly have taken a very different form, or might never have happened at all.
But Hitler did not act in a vacuum.
He came to power under very specific conditions, and those conditions were created by specific decisions of specific people, many of whom never intended the consequences.
The architects of the Treaty of Versailles created the resentment.
The bankers behind the Dawes Plan created the dependence.
The politicians who failed to confront aggression in Manchuria, Ethiopia and the Rhineland created the expectation that aggression goes unpunished.
And the statesmen who signed the Munich Agreement created the final illusion that war could be avoided by sacrificing the rights of smaller states.
The Second World War was not a natural disaster.
It was a man-made catastrophe, assembled over decades from vengefulness, greed, cowardice and deliberate looking away.
Its true origins lie not in the invasion of Poland in 1939, but in a chain of failures that began the moment the guns fell silent in 1918.
So the next time someone tells you the Second World War began on 1 September 1939, you will know the whole story.
It began twenty years earlier.
It began with a treaty that was meant to secure peace and instead prepared the ground for war.
It began with bankers who cared more about profits than stability.
It began with politicians who put appeasement above principle.
And it began with a world that looked away again and again, until it was too late to look away any longer.
History does not repeat itself, but it rhymes.
Economic crises, rising nationalism, weakened international institutions and rulers who test the limits and meet no resistance: this pattern has appeared again and again throughout history.
Recognising it may be the most important lesson of the most destructive war in human history.
