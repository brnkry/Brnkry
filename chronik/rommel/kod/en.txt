At noon on 14 October 1944, a dark car stops outside a house in Herrlingen near Ulm.
Two generals get out.
They do not stay long.
Shortly afterwards, the master of the house leaves with them and gets into the car.
A little later, he is dead.
Officially, he is said to have died of the effects of a wound.
There is a state funeral with full honours.
But that was a lie.
The man was Field Marshal Erwin Rommel, and hardly any German soldier has been written about, admired and argued over as much.
Who was he really, and how did he become a legend?
To understand Rommel, we have to go back to October 1917.
A young first lieutenant from Württemberg leads a small detachment through the mountains on the Italian front.
His soldiers have been on the move for days with almost no sleep.
They bypass positions instead of attacking them head-on, and appear where nobody expects them.
In the end, they stand on the summit of Monte Matajur.
By his own account, Rommel's unit took around 9,000 Italian soldiers prisoner in those days.
He received the Pour le Mérite, Prussia's highest award for bravery.
He was born in 1891 in Heidenheim an der Brenz, the son of a teacher.
Nothing pointed to a military family.
But it was these days in the mountains that shaped him: be fast, lead from the front, surprise the enemy.
Twenty years later he wrote a book about it.
It was called Infantry Attacks, became a bestseller and made him known all the way to the top leadership.
The book opened doors for him.
In 1939, during the attack on Poland, Rommel was responsible for the security of the supreme headquarters, and so was close to the head of state every day.
Afterwards he wanted a command of his own, and he got one that hardly anyone thought suited him: a panzer division.
Rommel was an infantryman and had never commanded tanks.
In May 1940, Germany attacks France.
Rommel's 7th Panzer Division crosses the Meuse at Dinant and pushes west so fast that at times even its own command does not know where it is.
Soon it is called the Ghost Division.
Once again Rommel leads from the front, often from a tank turret or a radio car.
His division reaches the Channel coast and later takes Cherbourg.
But the rapid advance also has a downside.
The 7th Panzer Division suffers heavy losses in this campaign, and Rommel tends to play down the achievements of neighbouring units.
Already here something begins that will follow him through the whole war: Rommel understands how important images are.
Cameras and war correspondents accompany him, and at home his name becomes famous.
In February 1941, Rommel lands in Libya.
The Italian troops are close to collapse, and Berlin sends a small German corps, the German Afrika Korps.
The orders are clear: defend and wait.
Rommel attacks anyway.
Within a few weeks he drives the British back to the Egyptian border.
Only one town holds out: Tobruk.
For months he besieges the port in vain.
In the desert, one thing counts above all: supplies.
Every litre of fuel and every shell has to be brought across the Mediterranean and then hundreds of kilometres along a single coastal road.
Rommel often plans further than his supplies can reach.
Even so, his reputation grows, on both sides.
In the British press he gets a nickname that will never leave him: the Desert Fox.
British soldiers talk about him so often that their commander-in-chief, Claude Auchinleck, sends a letter to his commanders.
In it he warns that Rommel is becoming a kind of magician or bogeyman to his own men.
They should no longer speak of Rommel, but simply of the Germans.
In June 1942, Tobruk finally falls, and more than 30,000 soldiers are taken prisoner.
Rommel is promoted to field marshal.
At 50, he is the youngest in the German army.
But Rommel has gone too far.
At El Alamein, about 100 kilometres from Alexandria, the advance grinds to a halt.
On the other side, Bernard Montgomery takes command, and the British receive more and more tanks, aircraft and fuel.
When the great battle begins in October 1942, Rommel is not even in Africa.
He is ill, taking a cure in Austria, and rushes back.
After twelve days it is clear that the front cannot be held.
Then an order arrives from headquarters: not one step back, victory or death.
Rommel hesitates for a day, then retreats anyway.
Half a year later, in May 1943, the German and Italian troops in Tunisia surrender.
Around 250,000 soldiers are taken prisoner.
By then, Rommel himself is no longer in Africa.
At the end of 1943, Rommel is given a new task.
He is to defend the French coast against the expected Allied landing.
He has millions of mines laid and obstacles set up on the beaches, slanted stakes and steel crosses meant to rip open landing craft.
In the meadows behind them stand wooden poles against gliders; the soldiers call them Rommel's asparagus.
Rommel is convinced the battle will be decided on the beach in the first 24 hours.
That is why he wants the tanks close to the coast.
Other generals want to hold them back further inland.
In the end there is a compromise that follows neither plan properly.
And on the morning of 6 June 1944, Rommel is not in Normandy.
He is at home in Herrlingen, because it is his wife Lucie's birthday.
A few weeks after the landing, Rommel considers the war lost and says so openly to his superiors.
On 17 July 1944, an Allied fighter plane attacks his car in Normandy.
Rommel is badly injured in the head.
Three days later, the assassination attempt of 20 July fails, and a great wave of arrests begins.
Rommel's name comes up too.
How much he really knew is disputed to this day.
What is certain is that men around him, such as his chief of staff Hans Speidel, were in contact with the conspirators.
Whether Rommel would have supported an assassination cannot be proven.
For the leadership in Berlin, suspicion was enough.
Which brings us back to Herrlingen, on 14 October 1944.
The two generals, Wilhelm Burgdorf and Ernst Maisel, bring Rommel a choice.
Either a trial before the People's Court, with consequences for his family and his staff, or poison.
If he chooses poison, his wife and son will be spared, and he will receive a state funeral.
Rommel says goodbye to Lucie and his 15-year-old son Manfred and gets into the car.
Just outside the village, he takes a cyanide capsule.
Four days later, Field Marshal Gerd von Rundstedt gives the funeral speech in Ulm.
He speaks of a hero who succumbed to his wounds.
A death mask is taken of Rommel's face.
For decades it lay in a display case of the Rommel Museum in Herrlingen, until the municipality closed the museum in 2019 because the exhibition was considered outdated.
The truth only comes out after the war, through Manfred Rommel, who later becomes mayor of Stuttgart.
After 1945, Rommel's fame keeps growing, above all through his former enemies.
Books about him appear in Britain, and Hollywood makes a film.
The image of a fair war in the desert suits the times: here, it seemed, there was no ideology, only two armies in the sand.
The new Bundeswehr also needs role models, and barracks are named after him.
Historians today take a much more critical view.
He owed his steep rise to the leadership of the time and was close to it for a long time.
In North Africa the war was seen as less brutal than in the East, but civilians suffered there too, and in Tunisia Jews under German occupation were forced into labour.
Other offices were responsible for this, not Rommel's staff.
How much he knew about it remains open.
And some of the mines his troops laid in North Africa are still in the ground today.
In his hometown of Heidenheim, the figure of a mine victim has stood next to his memorial since 2020.
An officer who led from the front and had a feel for images.
A general who disobeyed orders when he thought they were wrong, and who in the end was forced to his death by his own leadership.
