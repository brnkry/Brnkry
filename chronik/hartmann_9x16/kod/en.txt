He is credited with 352 aerial victories.
A Soviet court sentenced him to 25 years in prison.
And later he led the first jet fighter wing of the Bundeswehr.
Erich Hartmann.
Erich Hartmann was born in April 1922 in Weissach in Württemberg.
His father was a doctor, his mother Elisabeth a glider pilot.
Because of the severe economic situation, the family moved to China, where Erich grew up, until civil war broke out there in 1928 and they returned to Germany.
His mother taught him to fly, and at 14 he was already giving gliding lessons himself.
After finishing school, he joined the Luftwaffe in October 1940.
His training as a fighter pilot took almost two years and began with a setback.
In March 1942 he flew forbidden aerobatics over an airfield and was punished.
A comrade took over his replacement flight and was killed in a crash landing.
In autumn 1942 Hartmann joined Fighter Wing 52 on the Eastern Front.
His very first mission on 14 October ended in an emergency landing, because he had lost his flight leader and ran out of fuel before reaching the airfield.
The punishment was three days of ground duty.
After that, Flight Sergeant Edmund Roßmann became his teacher.
He taught him to keep his distance and to fire only from close range.
Hartmann's principle later was: "Fly with your head, not with your muscles."
In early November 1942 he reported his first victory, an Ilyushin Il-2. Debris damaged his aircraft, and he had to make another emergency landing.
Until spring 1943 Hartmann reported only a few victories.
Because of his youthful looks, his comrades called him "Bubi".
The name is said to come from Walter Krupinski, who kept telling him over the radio to get closer.
In the summer his numbers then rose quickly.
On 7 July, during the Battle of Kursk, he reported seven victories in a single day.
His method was: observe, decide, attack and break away.
In August, by his own account, he briefly fell into Soviet hands after an emergency landing and managed to escape.
On 2 September 1943 he took command of the 9th squadron of Fighter Wing 52. In total he had to make 16 emergency landings.
He was never shot down by an opponent in aerial combat.
On 29 October 1943, with 148 victories, he received the Knight's Cross.
One of his aircraft bore a painted heart for his future wife Ursula, known as "Usch".
In March 1944, at 202 victories, the Oak Leaves followed, and in July, at 268, the Swords.
On 24 August 1944 he reported eleven victories in one day, passing the 300 mark. Researchers view this number critically, because apparently the successes of the whole group were credited to him.
One day later, with 301 victories, he received the Diamonds, one of only 27 such awards.
Two weeks later, on 10 September 1944, he married Ursula Paetsch, whom he had known since school.
He was offered a transfer to the new jet fighters several times, and he declined.
On 8 May 1945 he reported his last victory, over Brünn.
In total he had flown 1,404 missions.
His unit then destroyed its aircraft and surrendered to American troops.
On 14 May 1945 the Americans handed Hartmann and his men over to the Red Army.
He refused to cooperate with the Soviets and, by his own account, was put in solitary confinement.
At the end of 1949 he was sentenced to 20 years in prison, and in 1951, in a second trial, to 25 years.
Among other things, he was accused of killing 780 civilians and attacking a bakery.
Hartmann denied this.
Today the trial is considered politically motivated.
His son Erich Peter was born in 1945 and died in 1948. Hartmann never saw him.
In October 1955 he was released with the last German prisoners of war.
In 1997 the Russian military prosecutor's office overturned the verdicts.
In 1956 he joined the new Bundeswehr and from 1957 was trained on jet aircraft in the USA.
From 1959 to 1962 he commanded Fighter Wing 71 "Richthofen", the first all-jet fighter unit of the West German Air Force.
He considered the Starfighter unsuitable and said so openly.
Later many of these aircraft crashed, and more than 100 German pilots died.
In 1970 he was retired early, probably partly because of this criticism.
After that he worked as a flight instructor in Hangelar near Bonn.
He died on 20 September 1993 at the age of 71 in Weil im Schönbuch.
Hartmann's second logbook was lost.
His biographers therefore compiled the later claims from other sources.
Some of these claims cannot be reconciled with Soviet loss records.
In 2005 the Russian researcher Dmitri Khazanov estimated the actual number at 70 to 80 victories.
Most historians do not follow this estimate.
The 1970 book "The Blond Knight of Germany" was criticized as one-sided by the historians Ronald Smelser and Edward Davies.
Hartmann was buried in the New Cemetery in Weil im Schönbuch.
